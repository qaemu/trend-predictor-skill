# Learning curves beat experts; timing still kills

The most accurate repeatable way to forecast a technology 5–10 years out is to extrapolate its own cost-per-unit-performance trend statistically, using Moore's or Wright's law with Farmer–Lafond error bands. In hindcasts across 50–60 technologies this method is well calibrated, and in head-to-head tests it beats both expert elicitation and the institutional models that analysts and governments rely on. Those institutional baselines lean consistently in two directions. They are too pessimistic about modular, mass-manufactured technologies: 2,905 integrated-assessment projections expected solar PV costs to fall 2.6%/yr over 2010–2020, and the actual decline was about 15%/yr. They are too optimistic about large, site-built, policy-dependent projects: only about 11% of announced 2030 low-emissions hydrogen is past final investment decision. What models cannot supply is *timing of adoption*. Base rates are sobering here: a median of 32 years from invention to widespread use for energy and consumer hardware, and 12–16 years from TRL 3 to TRL 9 for complex systems. Adoption usually stalls on complements, finance, permitting and supply chains rather than on the core technology. Judgmental tools (Delphi, superforecasting, prediction markets, scenarios, VC "why now" theses) do better at saying *whether* a change happens and *what could break the trend* than at saying *when*, and some of the most popular frameworks, such as the Gartner Hype Cycle, have little empirical support. A feasibility skill should therefore split each forecast into a modelled cost/performance core with explicit, widening bands and a judgmental adoption layer with base-rate priors, haircuts on announcements, and resolvable, scored predictions.

## Experience curves deliver calibrated cost forecasts, with bands that widen faster than √τ

The empirical foundation is a pair of Santa Fe Institute studies. Nagy, Farmer, Bui and Trancik hindcast six candidate laws on **62 technologies** from the Performance Curve Database. **Wright's law** (cost falls a fixed fraction per doubling of cumulative production) was slightly best, and **Moore's law** (cost falls exponentially with time) was "not far behind." Variation in rates *across technologies* was about an order of magnitude larger than the differences between laws ([Nagy et al. 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0052669)). The two laws nearly coincide because production itself grows exponentially (Sahal's identity: Moore drift μ = ω·r). They diverge only when production growth changes abruptly ([Lafond et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1703.05979)). Multi-parameter variants such as Nordhaus overfit and produced "large and inconsistent errors" out of sample ([Nagy et al. 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0052669)). The practical lesson is that getting the technology-specific rate right matters far more than choosing the law.

Farmer and Lafond turned this into a forecasting procedure with known error properties ([arXiv 1502.05274](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1502.05274v4); [Research Policy 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05274)). Log cost follows a random walk with drift and autocorrelated increments (IMA(1,1)). Drift μ̂ and volatility K̂ are estimated from the last *m* annual changes, and the point forecast is y_t + μ̂τ, which exponentiates to the *median* cost. The expected squared normalized error scales as **(m−1)/(m−3)·(τ + τ²/m)**. The τ term is accumulated shocks; the τ²/m term is drift-estimation error, so short series blow up long-horizon bands. With autocorrelation the authors recommend a pooled **θ = 0.63**. Setting θ = 0 understates errors by about a factor of two, and per-technology θ estimates are too noisy to use. Normalized errors follow a Student t with m−1 degrees of freedom. On **6,391 hindcasts across 53 technologies** the predicted error growth matched observed errors, with no significant bias out to about 11 years and some bias beyond, where data thin out. A useful cross-technology prior is that faster-improving technologies are more volatile (K̃ ≈ 0.02 − 0.76·μ̃, R² = 0.87). In the authors' worked solar example (μ̂ = −0.10/yr, K̂ = 0.15, m = 33), there was only about a 5% chance that the 2030 module price would be at or above its 2013 level.

What this means for the size of uncertainty: for a technology with K̂ = 0.15 and 20 years of data, the 95% band is roughly **×/÷2.7 around the median at 5 years and ×/÷4.7 at 10 years**. These are worked calculations from the published formulas, not figures the papers report. For the young, fast series typical of AI (m ≈ 5), the drift term doubles the variance multiplier at τ = 10 relative to m = 20.

Wright's law forecasts are *conditional* on a deployment path. Lafond et al. give the exact error variance, σ²(τ + (ΣX_future)²/ΣX_past²), which grows with the ratio of future to past log-experience growth ([Lafond et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1703.05979)). Way, Ives, Mealy and Farmer applied stochastic Wright's law (pooled autocorrelation ρ = 0.19, no floor costs) under exogenous logistic deployment scenarios. Their backtest covered about 50 technologies and 6,000 forecasts at horizons up to 20 years, and accuracy "closely matched" a priori estimates ([INET working paper](https://oms-inet.files.svdcdn.com/production/files/energy_transition_paper-INET-working-paper.pdf); [Oxford Martin School](https://www.oxfordmartin.ox.ac.uk/publications/empirically-grounded-technology-forecasts-and-the-energy-transition-2)). The working paper puts the expected saving of a Fast Transition over No Transition at about **$14 trillion** in net present value at a 1.4% discount rate. The published Joule figure is widely quoted as about $12 trillion, but that number was not verified in these notes. One gap remains: no published test exists of a *joint* forecast in which deployment itself is forecast rather than taken as realised, so the validated accuracy is conditional accuracy.

Learning rates vary widely, and they depend on the window and the metric:

| Technology (metric, window) | Learning rate per doubling | Source |
|---|---|---|
| PV modules, 1976–2008 / 2008–2019 | 20.8% / 38.8% | [Glenk et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41471-021-00114-8) |
| PV LCOE, 2010–2020 | ~40% | same |
| Onshore wind system price, 1983–2019 / LCOE 2010–2019 | ~9% / ~40% | same |
| Li-ion packs, 2010–2019 | 19.6% (literature 16–24%) | same |
| PEM electrolyzer systems, 2004–2019 | 9.9% | same |

Rubin et al.'s review found "as much as an order of magnitude" variability in reported rates for every electricity technology ([Rubin et al. 2015](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/enepol/v86y2015icp198-218.html)). As category priors, mass-manufactured modular products sit near 20% and field-built systems near 10%, but a technology's own series should override these whenever one exists. The NHGRI series shows how breakpoints work: sequencing costs began to "profoundly out-pace" Moore's law in January 2008, when second-generation platforms replaced Sanger sequencing ([NHGRI](https://www.genome.gov/about-genomics/fact-sheets/dna-sequencing-costs-data)).

AI is the extreme case. Epoch AI's trend estimates (updated February 2026) put frontier training compute at **about 5×/yr**, training cost at about 3.5×/yr, AI-chip FLOP/$ at about 1.49×/yr, and GPU FLOP/s per watt at 1.34×/yr ([Epoch AI Trends](https://epoch.ai/trends)). Compute needed to reach fixed language-model performance halves about every **8 months** (95% CI 5–14) ([Epoch, algorithmic progress](https://epoch.ai/blog/algorithmic-progress-in-language-models)). Price per token at fixed capability has fallen 9× to 900× per year depending on the milestone, and Epoch says the fastest recent declines are the least likely to persist ([Epoch, inference prices](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/llm-inference-price-trends)). Compute grows about 5×/yr while price-performance improves about 1.5×/yr, so spending must grow about 3.5×/yr. The constraint most likely to bind first is therefore capex, power and fab capacity, not a learning-curve floor. Gigawatt-scale data centres take about 2.1 years and about $38B per GW to build ([Epoch AI Trends](https://epoch.ai/trends)). No 5–10 year hindcast of AI trend extrapolation exists yet, because the series are too young.

## Ceilings bind performance, floors get breached, and S-curves lie before the inflection

Trend breaks are real. **Dennard scaling ended around 2005–2007**: clock speeds stalled near 4–6 GHz, the industry moved to multicore, and Intel cancelled Tejas and Jayhawk ([Wikipedia: Dennard scaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennard_scaling)). That break stalled one *attribute*, single-thread frequency, while cost per unit of computation kept improving through parallelism. The same pattern repeats across cases: performance attributes saturate, while cost-per-unit metrics often keep learning because the architecture changes underneath them.

Engineering cost floors fare poorly. The best-documented battery floor study used mineral costs to project NMC packs at only about **$124/kWh by 2030** and called sub-$100 unrealistic ([Hsieh et al. 2019, MIT](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/123880)). BNEF's 2025 survey found a volume-weighted average of **$108/kWh** already, with China at $84, stationary packs at $70, and the lowest LFP stationary packs at **$50/kWh** ([BNEF](https://about.bnef.com/insights/clean-transport/new-record-lows-for-battery-prices/)). The floor applied to one chemistry, and the market switched chemistries. IAM floor costs for PV have likewise been breached repeatedly, and Way et al. found no empirical support for imposing them ([INET working paper](https://oms-inet.files.svdcdn.com/production/files/energy_transition_paper-INET-working-paper.pdf)). Breaks also run in the fast direction: the PV module learning rate nearly doubled after 2008, partly because Chinese capacity expansion priced below long-run marginal cost ([Glenk et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41471-021-00114-8)).

Physical limits are best used as *ceilings on performance*, which bound how much headroom remains. Single-junction cells face the 33.7% Shockley–Queisser limit, with crystalline silicon's practical ceiling near 29.4%. A 27.03% commercial-size silicon cell record already leaves under 10% headroom ([Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/nature-index/article/10.1038/s41467-025-61128-y)), so further efficiency gains need a new architecture, such as LONGi's 33.9% perovskite–silicon tandem, which still has stability problems ([PV Europe](https://www.pveurope.eu/solar-modules/efficiency-record-longi-reports-339-cent-tandem-cells-made-silicon-and-perovskite)). Large wind turbines reach about 75–85% of the Betz limit ([Wikipedia: Betz's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betz%27s_law)). Computing, by contrast, ran about a billion times above the Landauer limit as of 2012, so that limit is irrelevant at a 10-year horizon ([Wikipedia: Landauer's principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landauer%27s_principle)).

S-curve fitting is the most abused method in this toolkit. Sandberg et al. show that logistic and other sigmoid fits reproduce past data well but forecast poorly unless the data span both sides of the inflection point. Early growth data carry almost no information about saturation, and failed forecasts such as peak oil and COVID-19 tended to place the inflection "near now" ([Sandberg et al., "Sigmoids behaving badly"](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.08065)). Even proponents restrict fits to curves that are already more than 95% complete ([Debecker & Modis 2021](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/tefoso/v163y2021ics0040162520312580.html)). The defensible practice is to fix the ceiling from outside the data (a physical limit or the addressable market), fit only the rate and midpoint, and present several ceilings as separate scenarios.

## Readiness and adoption take decades, and announcement pipelines overshoot

Technical readiness is slower and noisier than pitch decks imply. In an INCOSE analysis, GPS took **11.7 years from TRL 3 to 9** and a Ford voice-control product took about **16 years**. In a NASA set of about 20 aerospace technologies, single transitions sometimes exceeded 10 years, and early transition durations were strongly correlated (r ≈ 0.91 for log durations of TRL 2→3 and 3→4) ([INCOSE IS2012](https://www.incose.org/wp-content/uploads/legacy/presentation/IS2012-pdf/2-3-2.pdf)). That correlation is useful: a project's observed pace so far predicts its future pace. The samples are small, though, and no study with more than 100 cases of TRL 4→9 duration was found.

TRL also ignores commercial risk, which is why DOE built **Adoption Readiness Levels**. ARL scores 17 dimensions as low, medium or high risk across four areas: value proposition, market acceptance, resource maturity, and license to operate. The dimensions include delivered cost at scale, switching costs, the path to market, capital, manufacturing and supply chain, critical materials, permitting and public perception ([DOE OTT](https://www.energy.gov/technologytransitions/adoption-readiness-levels-arl-complement-trl); [CARAT](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2023-03/Commercial%20Adoption%20Readiness%20Assessment%20Tool%20%28CARAT%29_030323.pdf)). DOE applied it alongside TRL in its Liftoff reports ([DOE Liftoff LDES](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2023-09/Pathways%20to%20Commercial%20Liftoff%20Long%20Duration%20Energy%20Storage%20Opportunities_508.pdf)). DOE says the framework is still being refined. Definitions of MRL and ARENA's Commercial Readiness Index were not verified in these notes.

Historical base rates set the prior. Across 13 energy and consumer technologies, Gross et al. found a **median of 32 years from first prototype to widespread use** (20% of peak), split evenly at 18 years to market and 18 more to widespread use. Electricity-generation technologies took a median of 43 years, consumer end-use products 27, and replacement products 27 versus 35.5 for novel ones. The fastest market-to-widespread steps were 5–8 years (CCGT, VCR) ([Gross et al. 2018](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/87787/1/Gross-etal-EP2018-How-long-does-innovation-commercialisation-energy-sectors-take-historical-case-studies.pdf)). Cross-country adoption lags averaged about 45–47 years after invention; they have shrunk, but the depth of use has diverged across countries ([HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-technology-adoption-affects-global-economies)). The sample is heavy on energy and hardware, so these base rates should not be applied to software unchanged. For diffusion after launch, typical Bass parameters are p ≈ 0.03 and q ≈ 0.38 ([Wikipedia: Bass model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bass_diffusion_model), citing Mahajan, Muller & Bass 1995). These imply peak sales about 6 years after launch, with about 46% of potential adopters reached by then (calculation from the standard formulas). The hard parameter is market potential *m*, not p or q, and these notes found no evidence on how accurate pre-launch analogy-based Bass forecasts are.

Roadmaps and announcement pipelines are biased in predictable directions. An ex-post review of IEA World Energy Outlooks from 1993 to 2022 found renewable growth underestimated in every edition ([pv magazine 2025](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2025/04/11/ieas-world-energy-outlook-systemically-underestimates-solar-pv-development/)). The authors favour very high renewable shares, but the underestimate itself is widely documented. In the other direction, the IEA cut announced 2030 low-emissions hydrogen from 49 to "up to 37" Mt/yr, of which only about 4 Mt/yr is operational, under construction or past FID ([IEA 2025](https://www.iea.org/news/low-emissions-hydrogen-projects-are-set-to-grow-strongly-despite-wave-of-cancellations-and-persistent-challenges)). Fraunhofer ISI's Monte Carlo model, with 11 risk factors, expects only **54–75%** of the more than 2 TWh/yr of announced European battery cell capacity to be realised by 2030 ([Fraunhofer ISI](https://www.isi.fraunhofer.de/en/blog/themen/batterie-update/batterie-zell-produktion-europa-hochlauf-risiko-bewertung-gescheiterte-projekte.html)). Semiconductor roadmaps show a third behaviour: they redefine their targets. The 2014 ITRS projected gate-length shrinking to at least 2028; the final 2015 edition forecast it would stop around 2021, with progress shifting to 3D ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/transistors-will-stop-shrinking-in-2021-moores-law-roadmap-predicts)).

Scheduled events are the most forecastable inputs, but they slip too. In May 2026 the EU AI Act Digital Omnibus agreement moved Annex III high-risk obligations from 2 August 2026 to **2 December 2027**, and Annex I obligations to August 2028 ([Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/)). Patent expiries are hard dates, but they differ by jurisdiction. Semaglutide's core patent expired in early 2026 in China, India and Brazil and has lapsed in Canada, while an SPC holds in the EU to 2031 and US and Japanese protection is extended ([Reddie & Grose](https://www.reddie.co.uk/2026/02/09/changes-in-the-ozempic-patent-landscape/)).

## Judgment is good at "whether," models at "when"

Long-horizon judgmental forecasting has a thin and uncomfortable record. In Tetlock's *Expert Political Judgment*, experts barely beat equal-probability guessing, and simple extrapolation algorithms beat them. "Foxes" updated about 59% of the Bayes-prescribed amount after surprises, against about 19% for "hedgehogs." When foxes unpacked branching scenarios, their probabilities for mutually exclusive outcomes **summed to 2.09** ([Collins review of EPJ](https://blog.jcx.au/posts/tetlocks-expert-political-judgment-how-good-is-it-how-can-we-know)). Caplan's objection that the questions were unusually hard deserves weight. Open Philanthropy's review of 10+ year forecasting concluded "it is difficult to learn much of value" from past exercises, mainly because forecasts were too vague to score and had no baselines ([EA Forum](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/s8CwDrFqyeZexRPBP)). The best retrospective technology study, Fye et al. on the Mullins/DTIC dataset, found that only **method and horizon** mattered. Quantitative forecasts beat qualitative ones, expert methods were better at *whether* an event happened, shorter horizons were more accurate, timing errors were symmetric, and computing and robotics forecasts did best ([Fye et al. 2013](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/tefoso/v80y2013i6p1222-1231.html)). Kott and Perconti similarly found 1990s forecasts of military information technologies for about 2020 more accurate than forecasts of physical-effect technologies ([arXiv 1807.08339](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.08339)). The often-quoted superforecaster statistics from the IARPA ACE tournament were not verified in these notes and are omitted.

On costs, models beat experts directly. Meng, Way, Verdolini and Diaz Anadon compared 25 expert-elicitation datasets with four model-based methods. **Both underestimated cost declines.** Model medians were closer in 5 of 6 technologies, and model 5–95% ranges contained the observed 2019 value far more often at horizons up to 10 years ([CMCC](https://www.cmcc.it/article/recent-technology-cost-forecasts-underestimate-the-pace-of-technological-change); [PNAS 2021](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v118y2021pe1917165118.html)). LBNL's 140-expert wind survey shows the same anchoring: anticipated future costs were about **half** those in the 2015 survey, and earlier forecasts were "obsolete" ([LBNL](https://emp.lbl.gov/news/experts-predictions-future-wind)). Structured elicitation still adds value for drivers models cannot capture. The IDEA protocol (private estimate, aggregated feedback, discussion, second private estimate) improves second-round estimates, and self-rated expertise does not predict accuracy (ρ between −0.01 and −0.17, non-significant) ([BfR slides](https://www.bfr.bund.de/cm/349/the-idea-protocol-and-performance-weighting-for-expert-elicitation.pdf)).

Crowds are calibrated but modest. On 64 resolved AI binary questions, the Metaculus Community Prediction scored a Brier of 0.207 and the Metaculus Prediction 0.182, against 0.25 for always answering 50%. Perfect calibration could not be rejected ([EA Forum, Metaculus AI track record](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/e9htD7txe8RDdcehm/exploring-metaculus-s-ai-track-record)). That makes crowd forecasts good priors for 1–3 year milestones, but there are too few resolved 5–10 year questions to judge them at that range. Shell-style scenario planning makes no claim to predict. Wack's method separates predetermined elements from critical uncertainties so that mental models change before a discontinuity arrives. The evidence for it is narrative: Shell's refinery-upgrading policy paid off after 1973, but there is no comparison against competitors, and the authors say Shell only "seemed to have anticipated" the crisis ([Polytechnique Insights](https://polytechnique-insights.com/en/columns/society/case-study-how-shell-anticipated-the-1973-oil-crisis)). The **Gartner Hype Cycle** fares worse. The Economist found that perhaps a fifth of breakthrough technologies followed the full path, and about six in ten that entered the trough never recovered. A review of Gartner's charts since 2000 found most important technologies were never flagged early ([Wikipedia: Gartner hype cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gartner_hype_cycle), summarising The Economist 2024 and Mullany 2016). "In the trough" is at most a weak abandonment signal, not a timing tool.

## VCs bet on "why now," and science leads patents by about four years

Venture practice treats forecasting as a timing problem. Sequoia's template has a dedicated **Why Now** section: "why hasn't your solution been built before now?" ([Sequoia template](https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/sequoia-capital-business-plan-template/)). Bill Gross's analysis of 200 companies ranked **timing first at 42%**, ahead of team (32%) and idea (28%) ([Tech.co](https://tech.co/bill-gross-ted-2015-06)). That analysis is retrospective and self-scored by one incubator founder, and stories about VC timing carry heavy survivorship bias. Paul Graham's heuristic, "live in the future, then build what's missing," turns leading-edge use into idea generation ([paulgraham.com](https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)). ARK Invest is the clearest public thesis shop and grounds its cost forecasts in Wright's law ([ARK podcast](https://www.ark-invest.com/podcast/wrights-law-understanding-technology-cost-curves-with-brett-winton)). Its record has two sides. A 2018 Tesla target was reached about two years early. Its 2029 target of about $2,600 assumes robotaxis supply nearly 90% of value; without them the figure is about $350 ([Fortune 2024](https://fortune.com/2024/06/12/tesla-stock-price-predictions-cathie-wood-ark-invest-elon-musk)). Learning-curve calls hold up. Calls that hinge on one complementary breakthrough carry far more timing risk. Chris Dixon's "weekend hobbies" heuristic and Thiel's "secrets" question were not verified in these notes.

Bibliometric leading indicators give real but coarse lead times. Across 6,671 ERC-funded projects, about 40% were cited in patents, with an average lag of **3.7 years** from paper to first patent citation and some lags of 10–12 years ([Science|Business](https://sciencebusiness.net/news-byte/basic-research-takes-average-37-years-be-cited-patent-applications)). Patent and publication counts can place a technology on its life cycle, as with graphene's dated science and technology phases ([arXiv 1909.10256](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.10256); [IJISM 2023](https://ijism.isc.ac/article_705739.html)). The plausible sequence runs papers, then patents, then open-source activity, then funding and job postings. The lead times for GitHub activity, hiring and funding are *unverified*: no quantitative study was found. A useful hype tell is funding and news growth without matching growth in engineering papers and patents.

## Six failure modes recur, and they point in opposite directions

The documented errors are not a single "optimism bias." They are different biases for different technology types, which is why a single global correction fails.

| Failure mode | Direction | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Anchoring on today's cost for modular, fast-learning tech | Too pessimistic | IAM PV projections at 2.6%/yr vs ~15%/yr actual ([INET](https://oms-inet.files.svdcdn.com/production/files/energy_transition_paper-INET-working-paper.pdf)); experts and models both too high ([Meng et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v118y2021pe1917165118.html)) |
| Taking announcement pipelines at face value | Too optimistic | ~11% of 2030 hydrogen firm ([IEA](https://www.iea.org/news/low-emissions-hydrogen-projects-are-set-to-grow-strongly-despite-wave-of-cancellations-and-persistent-challenges)); 54–75% of EU battery capacity ([Fraunhofer](https://www.isi.fraunhofer.de/en/blog/themen/batterie-update/batterie-zell-produktion-europa-hochlauf-risiko-bewertung-gescheiterte-projekte.html)) |
| Treating engineering floors as hard | Too pessimistic | $124/kWh floor beaten by $108 average and $50 LFP ([MIT](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/123880); [BNEF](https://about.bnef.com/insights/clean-transport/new-record-lows-for-battery-prices/)) |
| Fitting S-curve asymptotes pre-inflection | Either; inflection "near now" | [Sandberg et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.08065) |
| Vivid scenarios and slow updating | Overconfidence | Probabilities summing to 2.09; hedgehogs update 19% ([Collins](https://blog.jcx.au/posts/tetlocks-expert-political-judgment-how-good-is-it-how-can-we-know)) |
| Forecasting effects, or ignoring complements | Timing errors | Effects forecast worse than technologies ([EA Forum](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/s8CwDrFqyeZexRPBP)); robotaxi-dependent target ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/06/12/tesla-stock-price-predictions-cathie-wood-ark-invest-elon-musk)) |

Unscorable vagueness sits under all six: most long-range forecasts cannot be checked, so forecasters never learn from them.

## Design implications for a 5- and 10-year constraint-projection skill

The evidence supports a two-layer architecture. The first layer is a statistical projection of each constraint's metric. The second is a judgmental and base-rate layer for adoption and complements. Feasibility is scored as the probability that the project's required thresholds are met in *each* layer at each horizon. The table lays out the modules; the reasoning follows.

| Module | Rule the skill applies | Grounding |
|---|---|---|
| Metric definition | Express each constraint as cost per unit performance (e.g. $/kWh, $/FLOP), with a stated physical ceiling or "none known" | [Farmer & Lafond](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1502.05274v4); [Landauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landauer%27s_principle) |
| Trend core | Default to Moore form; use Wright form only with explicit deployment scenarios (2–3, e.g. continue / logistic slowdown / stall) | [Nagy et al.](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0052669); [Lafond et al.](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1703.05979) |
| Bands | log SD = K̂·√(A*/(1+θ²)), θ = 0.63 (or ρ = 0.19 for Wright), t(m−1) quantiles, exponentiate; K̃–μ̃ prior if the series is short | [Farmer & Lafond](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1502.05274v4); [Way et al.](https://oms-inet.files.svdcdn.com/production/files/energy_transition_paper-INET-working-paper.pdf) |
| Breaks and ceilings | Cap upper performance bands at physical limits; treat cost floors as soft, tied to one chemistry or architecture; flag slowdown when μ̂ shifts by more than about 2 SE between windows | [Dennard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennard_scaling); [BNEF](https://about.bnef.com/insights/clean-transport/new-record-lows-for-battery-prices/) |
| S-curves | Never fit the asymptote pre-inflection; fix ceilings externally as scenarios | [Sandberg et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.08065) |
| Readiness prior | TRL 3–4 → 9 central estimate of 10–16 years for complex hardware; use observed past pace; score the 17 ARL dimensions separately | [INCOSE](https://www.incose.org/wp-content/uploads/legacy/presentation/IS2012-pdf/2-3-2.pdf); [DOE ARL](https://www.energy.gov/technologytransitions/adoption-readiness-levels-arl-complement-trl) |
| Adoption prior | Gross base rates for hardware (market to 20% of peak usually more than 5 years); a sub-5-year path needs a named reason (software, drop-in replacement, mandate) | [Gross et al.](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/87787/1/Gross-etal-EP2018-How-long-does-innovation-commercialisation-energy-sectors-take-historical-case-studies.pdf) |

Several choices in this design are judgment calls rather than published rules, and the skill should treat them as tunable. These include the 70–85% holding probability for statutory dates, the materials-share heuristic (a floor is unlikely to bind below about 30–50% materials share and likely above about 70%), and the haircut for hydrogen.

**Feasibility as a threshold crossing.** The core computation is a probability of crossing. For each constraint, the skill computes P(metric reaches the project's required value by year 5 and year 10) from the band distribution. It does the same for each competing incumbent; Farmer and Lafond's solar-versus-flat-competitor crossover is the template. The technical-economic score then combines the core constraints. Because constraints are not independent, the skill should report the weakest link and the joint probability side by side rather than one product. The adoption score multiplies by readiness and adoption priors and the ARL risk flags.

**Shrinkage toward base rates.** The evidence says forecasts of *effects* and of *physical* technologies are less accurate than forecasts of the technologies themselves and of information technologies ([Kott & Perconti](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.08339); [Fye et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/tefoso/v80y2013i6p1222-1231.html)). The skill should therefore shrink 10-year probabilities toward base rates more strongly than 5-year ones, and shrink harder for energy, materials and bio constraints than for compute. Scenario spread and statistical spread should be shown separately, because over 10 years disagreement between deployment scenarios often exceeds within-scenario noise.

**Methods not covered.** Cost-parity analysis is covered here only through the Farmer–Lafond crossover and Way et al.'s conditional forecasts. Techno-economic analysis was not researched in these notes. NREL-style bottom-up models, SunShot and Hydrogen Shot track records, MRL and CRI definitions, superforecaster tournament statistics, and the lead times of GitHub, hiring and funding indicators were flagged as unverified and are excluded. They should be sourced before the skill relies on them.

## Conclusion

Prediction is not the bottleneck it appears to be. For the cost and performance of an improving technology, a two-parameter random walk with honest bands already beats expert panels and institutional models, and its errors are known in advance. The unforecastable part is narrower and harder: whether complements, capital, permits and supply chains show up on time. Every method that claims to time that part, whether hype cycles, fitted S-curves or announcement pipelines, has a poor or negative record. A credible feasibility skill should therefore present its 10-year cost projection as the solid part of its answer and its adoption timing as the weak part, and say so.

The asymmetric biases are also an opening. Consensus tends to be too pessimistic about modular, mass-manufactured technologies and too optimistic about large, site-built ones, so a skill that applies type-specific corrections and scores its own resolvable predictions over time will find mispriced opportunities that consensus misses. That is the empirical basis for a "why now" thesis, as opposed to the narrative one.
